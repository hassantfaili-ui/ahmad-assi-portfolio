Ahmad Assi Website: low-fidelity wireframes, v1

Structure only. Greyscale, no typeface personality, no colour, no real copy. Every grey box marked with a cross is an image slot; every grey bar is a line of text yet to be written.

Seven screens: five desktop, two mobile. Each numbered pin maps to a note in the list under that screen.

How to give feedback: reference the pin, for example 1.4 or 3.6, and say what should change: move it, cut it, make it bigger, split it in two. Structural changes are cheap at this stage and expensive after the visual design is applied.

Screen 1 notes

  1. Header: wordmark left, four links right (Work, About, Contact, and CV). Stays put while scrolling or scrolls away, your call.
  2. The only thing above the fold: his name, what he does, and two actions. No hero image. Deliberate, because with placeholder imagery a hero slot invites a weak stock photo, and because a resume-first site should lead with the person. Flag this if you want a hero image instead, it is the single biggest open structural question on this screen.
  3. Four scannable facts a hiring firm looks for first: availability, location, credential or licence status, years of experience. Cut any that do not apply.
  4. Selected work, six projects maximum, three across. Curated subset, not everything. Each card is image, project name, and one line of meta such as type and year. Link out to the full index.
  5. Experience: dates in a narrow left column, role and firm plus two or three lines of contribution on the right. Reverse chronological. This is the section that makes it a resume rather than a gallery.
  6. Education on the left, software and skills as tags on the right, with languages and registrations below. Tags are the honest format here: firms scan for specific software names.
  7. One contact prompt before the footer so a visitor who has read the whole page does not have to scroll back up.
  8. Footer: contact details, page links, professional social. Anything he wants findable without hunting.

Screen 2 notes

  1. Page title with one or two lines framing how the work is organized. Short.
  2. Category filters, client-side, no page reload, no results count theatre. Categories are placeholders: the real ones depend on what he has built, and a student or early-career set may need Academic and Competition instead of Commercial and Cultural. Filters only appear once there are enough projects to justify them.
  3. Uniform grid, three across, consistent 4:3 crop so the page stays calm regardless of the source image shapes. Every card links to its own page. Cards carry name plus type and year only, no description: the grid is for choosing, not reading.

Screen 3 notes

  1. Back link to the work index, then the project name. No image above the title, so the page announces what it is before it performs.
  2. Lead image, wide crop, inset rather than full-bleed to the window edge. Full-bleed is a reasonable alternative and a genuine fork: full-bleed is more dramatic, inset keeps the whole site on one consistent margin.
  3. Brief on the left, fact table on the right: year, location, type, size, status, and his role. Firms read the table before the prose, so it must be scannable and identical in order on every project.
  4. Image pair, portrait crops, with one shared caption. Captions matter for architecture and are frequently forgotten.
  5. Drawings get their own slot and a light background, because plans and sections are usually line work on white and need different treatment from photography. This is the section most portfolio templates omit and the one hiring firms most want to see.
  6. An explicit statement of what he personally did, separate from the project description. On team projects this is the difference between honest and misleading, and it is what a reviewer is actually assessing.
  7. Remaining images, square crops, three across. Repeats as needed. The CMS should let him choose a block layout per image group rather than forcing one rhythm.
  8. Previous and next project, so a reviewer never dead-ends and has to use the back button.

Screen 4 notes

  1. Portrait plus the long-form bio the home page compresses into one line, then the CV download as a real PDF. Firms still want the PDF; the site does not replace it. If he has no portrait he is willing to use, say so and this becomes a single text column rather than a two-column layout with an empty slot.
  2. Full experience history, unabridged, in the same dates-left format as the home page. Home shows three roles, this shows all of them.
  3. Three supporting columns: education and credentials, awards and exhibitions, publications and press. Any that are empty get cut rather than shown empty. Tell me which of these three actually have content, or all three stay as placeholders.

Screen 5 notes

  1. Direct details first: a hiring manager will email rather than fill a form, so the address must be visible and copyable, never hidden behind the form.
  2. Four fields, no more. Success and error states shown inline on the same page, no redirect to a thank-you page. Spam protection without a CAPTCHA, since we cannot ask visitors to solve puzzles to reach him.

Screen 6 notes

  1. Menu collapses to a button opening a full-screen overlay. Four links do not justify a drawer with animation.
  2. Same order as desktop, nothing hidden on mobile. Buttons go full width and stack.
  3. Facts strip becomes a two-by-two grid rather than shrinking four columns into unreadable slivers.
  4. Work grid becomes a single column. Three visible on mobile rather than six, because vertical scroll cost is real, with the rest behind the index link.
  5. Experience switches from two columns to stacked, dates above the role. The narrow-left-column pattern collapses at this width.

Screen 7 notes

  1. Lead image drops from a 21:9 crop to 4:3, because a wide crop at 390 wide is a letterbox sliver.
  2. Fact table moves above the brief on mobile, inverting the desktop reading order. It is the densest useful information and should not sit below several paragraphs on a phone.
  3. Brief follows the table.
  4. All images become one column. Drawings keep the wider crop since line work needs the horizontal room, and get pinch-to-zoom, which matters more than anything else on this screen.
  5. Next project only, not previous and next, to keep the footer area simple.
